Chapter 01

First, forget the rules.

The first thing you should understand about songwriting is that there isn't one correct way to write a song.

You can write about love, anger, happiness, heartbreak, or something completely ridiculous. You can tell a story, describe a moment, or write something that makes absolutely no sense to anyone but you.

The important thing is to start writing.

One of the first mistakes I made when I started songwriting was trying to tell an entire story that unfolded over time. What I should have written about was something as simple as how I got out of bed that morning. LOL.

You have far less room to tell a story in a song than you might think. Sometimes one moment, one thought, or one feeling is enough to create an entire song.

You don't have to explain everything

Another mistake I made was being so concerned about expressing my pain that I thought everything needed to make sense. But sometimes there aren't words that adequately describe what you're feeling.

That's when you can paint a picture with your lyrics.

Instead of explaining that you're lonely, you might describe waking up and reaching for someone who isn't there. You've communicated the feeling without ever saying you're lonely.

Sometimes showing someone what a feeling looks like is more powerful than explaining the feeling itself.

Sometimes you're only writing for yourself

Maybe you're upset. Maybe you're happy. Maybe you're carrying something emotionally that you don't know what to do with.

Write it down. Don't worry about rhyming. Don't worry about structure. Don't even worry about making sense.

You can always go back and change it later. Or you can leave it exactly as it is.

Not everything you write needs to become a song for other people. Sometimes writing is simply how you get something out of yourself. And that's a perfectly good reason to write.

Chapter 02

Who are you writing for?

When you're writing something you hope other people will enjoy, there are a few things worth considering.

  • Relatability: Give people something they can emotionally connect with. They don't necessarily need to understand your personal experience to recognize the feeling behind it.
  • Simplicity: Don't bury your message beneath so many words that listeners lose the emotion you're trying to communicate.
  • Singability: People often enjoy music they can sing along with, whether they're driving, exercising, or listening at home.
  • Imagery: Give listeners something they can visualize or experience in their imagination.
  • Memorability: A strong phrase, unusual image, or catchy melodic pattern can stay with someone long after a song ends.

But here's where songwriting becomes interesting. Some incredibly successful songs don't tell straightforward stories.

Take Glycerine by Bush. Its lyrics are abstract, yet the music and emotional delivery make it memorable. Counting Crows sometimes uses dense, word-heavy lyrics, yet their musical phrasing can make those lyrics enjoyable to sing. And Fast Car by Tracy Chapman tells an emotionally complicated story through relatively direct language and vivid moments.

All three demonstrate different approaches.

A song doesn't necessarily have to make literal sense. It needs to create an experience.

You can also abandon traditional structure entirely. An acoustic fingerpicking song might tell one continuous story from beginning to end, without a repeating chorus or obvious hook.

This is often described as through-composed songwriting when the music develops continuously rather than relying on repeated sections. The story itself can become the reason someone keeps listening.

Chapter 03

Write first. Refine later.

Write your song however feels natural to you. When you're finished, or whenever you get stuck, come back and consider these questions:

  1. Am I trying to communicate too many things at once?
  2. Could one image or moment communicate this feeling better?
  3. Are there words I could remove without changing the meaning?
  4. Does the line feel natural when I say or sing it out loud?
  5. Could I change the wording, rhythm, or imagery to make it stronger?

What is a syllable, anyway?

A syllable is a beat or unit of sound within a spoken word. Love has one syllable, broken has two, and tomorrow has three.

Syllable count is simply the total number of syllables in one line. It can be a helpful way to spot a line that might be crowded or difficult to sing.

“I hear the rain” — 4 syllables
“I can hear the pouring rain” — 7 syllables

Neither line is automatically better. A melody might make room for the longer one, or the shorter one might deliver the feeling more powerfully.

You can have two lines with different syllable counts that sound perfectly natural together. And you can have two lines with identical counts that sound awkward together.

Why? Because songwriting isn't just about how many syllables are there. It's also about which syllables are emphasized, the duration of their sounds, and how the words fit the rhythm.

With practice, you begin to feel those patterns without consciously counting. Your ears and even your mouth begin recognizing when something doesn't fit. Counting is a tool for diagnosing a problem, not a law for writing lyrics.

A useful habit

Read your line aloud. If you keep stumbling or rushing, try removing a word, changing the word order, or moving the emphasis. Don't sacrifice the meaning just to make the numbers match.

Chapter 04

Songwriting techniques in action.

The following song was written to demonstrate different songwriting techniques. Each two-line group emphasizes a particular tool, while all the groups combine into one continuous story.

Read the whole song first, without worrying about the technical labels. Then we'll look at why the lines work.

A demonstration song

The House That Stole My Heart

A gothic story in eleven couplets (two-line groups)

The moon was a button sewn into the night,
I pulled at its thread and unraveled the light.

Soft shoes shuffled through shadow and snow,
Six silent stairways sank somewhere below.

Down the cold, old road, I followed the glow,
Where old bones hummed songs only ghosts ought to know.

The hearth held a fire that froze me to bone,
A room full of people, yet I stood alone.

The candles danced against the windowpane,
The rain had learned to sing a sad refrain.

The clock went tick, then quick, then slow,
I ran from the chime, but it wouldn't let go.

Above my head, the ceiling held the stars,
Their silver light spilled halfway down the stairs.

I found my name already verified,
I signed the guestbook, sweetly terrified.

She said I'd lost my heart somewhere upstairs,
I found it in a drawer marked “Small Repairs.”

The latch clicked shut. The floorboards cracked.
One breath. One beat. The dark breathed back.

I left the key upon the windowsill.
By sunrise, the house was gone, but its heartbeat followed me.

Chapter 05

Why the lines work.

Now that you've read the uninterrupted song, let's look at the tools hidden inside it.

01 · Imagery + Metaphor

“The moon was a button sewn into the night...”

Imagery creates a picture in the listener's imagination. A metaphor describes one thing as another to create an association.

The moon isn't literally a button, and the night isn't fabric. But the image gives you something concrete to see. Pulling the thread in the next line extends that metaphor.

02 · Alliteration + Consonance

“Soft shoes shuffled through shadow and snow...”

Alliteration repeats consonant sounds at the beginnings of nearby words. Consonance repeats consonant sounds in nearby words, including in the middle or at the ends.

The repeated S and SH sounds create a soft, shuffling texture. The sound of the words contributes to the atmosphere.

03 · Assonance + Internal Rhyme

“Down the cold, old road, I followed the glow...”

Assonance repeats vowel sounds. Hear the long O in cold, old, road, and glow.

Internal rhyme occurs within a line, as with cold and old. Meanwhile, glow and know rhyme at the ends of the lines. These patterns make the words feel connected.

04 · Antithesis + Paradox + Juxtaposition

“The hearth held a fire that froze me to bone...”

Antithesis contrasts opposing ideas. A paradox seems contradictory but may reveal a deeper meaning. Juxtaposition places contrasting things near each other.

A fire that freezes and a crowded room where someone still feels alone convey emotional disconnection without explaining it directly.

05 · Meter + Prosody + Perfect Rhyme

“The rain had learned to sing a sad refrain.”

Meter is a recurring pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables. This line approximates iambic pentameter, the five-beat rising pattern often associated with Shakespeare:

the RAIN | had LEARNED | to SING | a SAD | reFRAIN

Prosody concerns how language's stresses and phrasing interact with musical rhythm and meaning. Windowpane and refrain provide a perfect end rhyme. You don't need to use strict meter, but understanding how emphasis works helps you hear why a phrase flows.

06 · Internal Rhyme + Rhythmic Variation

“The clock went tick, then quick, then slow...”

Tick and quick rhyme inside one line. The short, sharp sounds followed by the longer vowel of slow suggest a change in pacing.

And tick is onomatopoeia, a word that imitates a sound. The language itself can become part of the percussion.

07 · Slant Rhyme

“Above my head, the ceiling held the stars...”

Stars and stairs are a slant rhyme (or near rhyme). They share a similar consonant pattern but have different stressed vowels.

Near rhymes let you keep the word you actually want instead of forcing a perfect rhyme that weakens the line.

08 · Multisyllabic Rhyme

“I found my name already verified...”

Verified and terrified rhyme over multiple syllables rather than only one. Multisyllabic rhyme can make phrases memorable and rhythmically satisfying, especially in word-dense styles.

09 · Wordplay + Double Meaning

“She said I'd lost my heart somewhere upstairs...”

Is the heart physically missing, or has the narrator lost the ability to love? The line works both ways.

That's double meaning, a form of wordplay. A related idea, double entendre, is a phrase with two interpretations, often with a humorous or suggestive second meaning.

10 · Phonetics + Cadence

“The latch clicked shut. The floorboards cracked.”

Phonetics is the study of speech sounds and how they're produced and perceived. Hard consonants give these lines a percussive edge.

Cadence is the rhythmic flow and resolution of a phrase. One breath. One beat. stops and starts, creating tension without needing extra words.

11 · Free Verse + Storytelling

“By sunrise, the house was gone, but its heartbeat followed me.”

The ending abandons regular rhyme and meter. Free verse doesn't follow a fixed metrical or rhyme scheme.

Now the story matters more than the pattern. The final image leaves the listener free to decide what the heartbeat means.

Chapter 06

Your songwriting toolbox.

You don't need to memorize these terms. Use this list when you get stuck, want new ideas, or sense that a line needs pruning.

Rhythm and sound

  • Prosody: How natural language stress and phrasing interact with musical rhythm.
  • Meter: A recurring pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables.
  • Cadence: The rhythmic flow and sense of arrival or resolution in a phrase.
  • Phonetics: Speech sounds and how they're formed and heard.
  • Syllable count: The number of syllables in a line. Helpful for spotting rhythm problems, but not a rule about how many you must use. See Chapter 3.
  • Alliteration: Repeated initial consonant sounds, as in wild winds whisper.
  • Assonance: Repeated vowel sounds, as in cold stone road.
  • Consonance: Repeated consonant sounds, as in blank and think.
  • Onomatopoeia: Words that imitate sounds, such as tick, buzz, crack.

Rhyme techniques

  • Perfect rhyme: Matching stressed vowel and following sounds, as in light/night.
  • Slant rhyme: Similar but imperfect sounds, as in stars/stairs.
  • Internal rhyme: Rhyming within a line, as in cold, old road.
  • Multisyllabic rhyme: Rhyming over multiple syllables, as in verified/terrified.
  • End rhyme: Rhyming words at the ends of lines.

Meaning and expression

  • Imagery: Language that creates sensory experiences or mental pictures.
  • Metaphor: Describing one thing as another to create an association.
  • Simile: Comparing things using like or as.
  • Antithesis: Contrasting opposing ideas.
  • Juxtaposition: Placing contrasting images or ideas close together.
  • Paradox: An apparent contradiction that may reveal a deeper meaning.
  • Wordplay: Using sounds or multiple meanings of words creatively.
  • Double entendre: Two possible interpretations, often with one humorous or suggestive.
  • Symbolism: Using an object or image to represent a larger idea.

Structure and storytelling

  • Hook: A memorable lyrical or musical phrase.
  • Repetition: Reusing words or phrases to create familiarity or emphasis.
  • Refrain: A recurring lyric or phrase that returns throughout a song.
  • Free verse: Writing without fixed rhyme or meter.
  • Narrative songwriting: Using lyrics to tell a story.
  • Through-composed songwriting: Music that develops continuously instead of relying heavily on repeated sections.

Try this before rewriting

Sometimes a line sounds wrong because it contains too many words. Sometimes the stress falls in the wrong place. Sometimes you're forcing a rhyme. Say or sing it aloud. Often you can hear the problem before you can explain it.

Chapter 07 · Optional platform advice

Turning lyrics into music with Suno.

Everything so far applies to songwriting generally. This section is about organizing lyrics for an AI music platform, where even good lyrics can come out sounding flat if the musical delivery doesn't vary.

Suno doesn't always interpret section labels exactly as a human songwriter or studio musician would. Labels such as [Bridge] can sometimes act as instructions encouraging a change in melody, rhythm, instrumentation, or delivery. They are cues, not guaranteed commands.

For example, a traditional bridge often appears later in a song to introduce contrasting material. With Suno, you might experiment with a bridge much earlier simply to encourage a musical shift.

What can a song section do?

Rather than treating line counts as rules, think about the job of each section:

[Verse]

Introduces or continues an idea. Could be four lines, six lines, or eight, depending on what you're telling and how it sounds.

[Pre-Chorus]

Often builds tension or momentum before a chorus. Completely optional.

[Chorus]

Often carries the main hook. Repetition helps it become recognizable.

[Bridge]

Traditionally a contrasting passage; in Suno, also worth experimenting with as a cue for musical variation, even earlier in a song.

[Post-Chorus]

An optional section after the chorus that can introduce another hook or musical texture.

[Breakdown]

Typically suggests a more noticeable change in energy, rhythm, or instrumentation.

[Intro] / [Outro]

Opening and closing sections. For Suno, use [Intro] cautiously if the opening words are essential to your story, since it may treat the intro as primarily musical.

One possible arrangement

Verse → Bridge → Pre-Chorus → Chorus → Verse → Bridge → Pre-Chorus → Chorus → Breakdown → Final Chorus.

That's an example, not a requirement. Some songs go straight from a verse to a chorus. Others use no pre-chorus, a single bridge, or no chorus at all.

Notice that this song doesn't follow a consistent number of lines, words, or syllables. The first verse begins with two lines, then expands into five more; the other sections have their own shapes. The shorter lines create space, the longer lines carry more information, and the repeating phrases connect the story. What matters is how the words work together when performed.

Why a long verse can sound flat

Imagine putting twenty-four lines under a single [Verse 1] label. The model may keep using similar vocal phrasing for the entire section. You may not immediately recognize the issue as a lack of variation. You'll simply feel that something isn't working.

Try breaking the lyrics into smaller labeled sections, experimenting with a bridge or pre-chorus, or regenerating the music. Sometimes the problem isn't the lyrics. It's how the platform chose to perform them.

A Suno-specific reminder

Shorter lines and section labels can help guide a generation, but they don't guarantee any particular result. Don't change lyrics you love just to fit a template. Experiment with the arrangement first.

Chapter 08

The most important lesson.

There are three different things involved in creating music:

Expression: Writing what you want or need to say.

Connection: Making something other people can feel, enjoy, remember, or relate to.

Production: Turning those words and ideas into the musical experience you want.

You don't have to accomplish all three at the same time.

Start with expression. If you're creating music for an audience, consider connection. When you're ready to produce, think about structure and arrangement.

And if you're just writing for yourself, you can ignore all of it.

You don't need to rhyme. You don't need a chorus. You don't need four lines in every section.

You can write one continuous story over a fingerpicked guitar, create an abstract song filled with strange imagery, or repeat four simple words until they become unforgettable.

The more techniques you learn, the more options you have.

You can find a thousand songwriting rules and another thousand songs that break every one of them. And both can sound incredible.

Write what you want to write. Learn why things work. Then decide for yourself which rules are worth keeping.
